# Chance Academy

A 60-day science, math and reading curriculum built for one specific
seven-year-old: inquisitive, loves engineering, walks the woods looking for
salamanders, fishes, and got hooked hard on the solar system during July
summer school. It runs in the browser on his iPad, takes **5–10 minutes a
day**, and reads every word out loud — in your own recorded voice if you want,
or a synthesized one until you do.

Open [`chance/index.html`](chance/index.html), or tap **CHANCE ACADEMY** on the
arcade menu.

---

## Put it on his iPad as an app icon

1. Open the Pages URL for this repo in **Safari** (not Chrome — Safari has the
   speech engine and the microphone).
2. Go to **Chance Academy**.
3. Tap **Share → Add to Home Screen**.

He now has a real app icon (a ringed planet over a green hill) that launches
fullscreen with no address bar and **works with no signal** — in the truck, at
the creek, anywhere. All 60 days are cached on the first visit.

One thing to do together the first time: tap **🔊 Test the voice** in the
grown-ups area and pick the voice you like. iOS will also ask once for
microphone permission the first time he taps the 🎤 button — say yes, that is
what checks his pronunciation.

## What a day looks like

Everything is driven by pictures. He never has to read a menu to get started:
one big card for today's mission, six picture tiles, and a small grown-up door
in the corner. Each mission has the same three stops, so the shape is
predictable and his attention goes to the content:

| | Stop | About 2–3 minutes |
|---|---|---|
| 🔭 | **Science** | A science idea in three narrated sentences, a "big word" with its pronunciation, then **one reasoning question** |
| 🔢 | **Numbers** | **Three real-world problems** — time/distance/speed, geometry, algebra, physics, or groups-of |
| 📖 | **Read & Say** | Three phonics words he **says out loud into the microphone**, a sentence built from the science, and one comprehension question |

Finish a stop, earn a ⭐. Finish all three, the day is stamped on the map. Get
every answer right on the first try and you earn a 💎 Clear Thinker gem.

**He is never trapped in a lesson.** Two buttons sit at the bottom of every
screen inside a mission — while the lesson reads, while a question is on screen,
and after he answers it:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| 🔇 | **Stop** | Hushes the talking instantly and stays where it is. For when the owl is mid-sentence and he is ready to move on |
| 🏠 | **Home** | Leaves for the picture menu. Asks "Stop for now?" first, so a stray thumb doesn't wipe out a half-finished station |

They are deliberately small next to the wide green button, so what to do *next*
is still the obvious thing to tap. The little 🏠 in the top corner asks the same
question.

The other tiles: **Mission Map** (all 60 days, jump to any one), **My Rewards**
(badges, goals, and the share button), **Word Wall** (all 180 phonics words,
tap any one to hear it sounded out).

## The math is real, and it is about the real world

Nothing is a bare worksheet sum. Every problem is a story he could stand
inside, and the domains rotate so he keeps meeting the same ideas in new
clothes:

- **Time, distance, speed** — walking to the creek at 2 mph, floating a stick
  down the creek to measure the current, counting seconds between lightning
  and thunder to get the miles, the space station's 90-minute lap.
- **Geometry** — quarter turns and degrees, area as rows-times-columns,
  perimeter vs area as two different jobs (fence around the garden vs seeds
  inside it), why a circle has no corners.
- **Algebra** — missing-number boxes, function machines ("put in 3, get 18 —
  what is the rule?"), balance-scale equations, extending patterns from his own
  test data.
- **Physics** — a rock and a pebble land together, force pairs on a skateboard,
  levers trading force for distance, buoyancy, stored energy in a catapult.
- **Groups-of** — his multiplication tables, embedded everywhere: 4 legs on
  5 salamanders, 7 nights for 3 weeks, 40 bugs to feed one big fish.

## Scan his homework and turn it into a lesson

Tap **📸 Scan Homework** on the home screen. The big yellow button opens the
iPad's camera.

1. **Take a picture** of the worksheet: flat, in good light, filling the screen.
2. **The app reads the words off the page by itself** in about 3 to 10 seconds.
   The reading happens on the iPad. Nothing is uploaded, and it works without
   wifi once it has been used once.
   - If the page has a numbered list ("1. light 2. night …"), it takes just
     those words, in the teacher's order, even when the list runs in two
     columns. The title, "Name / Date" and the instructions are skipped.
   - Math problems on the page ("7 x 8 =") are picked up as well.
3. **A grown-up checks the list.** Each word has a tick box and can be edited.
   - Untick anything that isn't a homework word, and fix any misread word.
   - Use *Add a word…* for any word it missed.
   - Words it wasn't sure about are marked *unsure* and arrive unticked.
   - Check the math signs: it sometimes reads ÷ as +.
4. Tap **▶️ Start the lesson** to begin.

The lesson Chance gets:

| Part | What he does |
|---|---|
| **Read it** | Each word appears with the worksheet photo above it. The owl sounds it out in chunks and he says it into the microphone. |
| **Spell it** | A spelling test. The owl says *"Which is the right way to spell: night?"* and he picks from three spellings: the right one and two realistic mistakes (*nite*, *nihgt*). The photo is hidden for this part, because the list would give the answers away. The wrong spellings are **never read aloud**, so he isn't trained on them. |
| **Math** | Any problems from the page, with the same tagged wrong answers as the course. |

The wrong spellings come from the mistakes second-graders actually make, not
random letter swaps:
- writing it how it sounds: *lite*, *bote*, *fone*
- mixing up vowel teams: *rain → rayn*
- dropping a silent letter: *nock*, *rite*
- getting a double letter wrong
- swapping letters in the tricky middle

Picking the sound-it-out spelling gets its own coaching from the owl: *"That's
how it sounds, but this word uses a special spelling. Look closely at the
letters in the middle."* It also shows up in the Thinking Report as **Spells it
the way it sounds**.

**What it reads well:** printed worksheets, which is what spelling lists almost
always are. **What it reads badly:** handwriting. If a page won't scan, tap
*Type the words instead*.

Each scanned lesson is saved under **Homework to study again**, so he can
repeat Monday's list on Thursday before the test.

### Building a worksheet by hand (Grown-Ups → 📸 Homework from school)

For anything the scanner can't handle, the hand-built path still exists:

1. Photograph the pages.
2. Type the problems in one box: `7x8, 6x7, 24/3, 15-8`.
3. Add spelling words (one per line) and any other question, with your own
   right and wrong answers.

### The part that makes this worth doing

A generated math question is not a bare answer key. `7x8` produces:

| Answer | Why it's offered |
|---|---|
| **56** | correct |
| 15 | he added instead of multiplying — tagged `additive-for-multiplicative` |
| 49 | he skip-counted one group short — tagged `off-by-one` |
| 63 | he reached for the bigger number — tagged `bigger-number-bias` |

So the live coaching and the Thinking Report work on his real homework exactly
as they do on the course. If he adds when he should multiply on Friday's
worksheet, that shows up in the same report as the same habit in the space
lessons — which is the whole point of tracking habits instead of scores.

Spelling words go through the same phonics table as the course: chunked, with
anchored sounds and the microphone check. The automatic split is good but not
perfect on irregular words, so you can force it by typing hyphens:
`sh-ad-ow`.

**A worksheet with no questions typed in still works.** He sees the photo, does
it on paper, and taps "I finished it". Sometimes that is all you have time for.

Homework earns stars and feeds the report, but it does **not** consume a day of
the 60-day course — it is extra, not a substitute.

## No sound?

Every lesson is read aloud, so silence is the worst thing that can go wrong — and
from the outside every cause looks the same. Three ways to fix it, fastest first:

1. **Tap 🔊** (top corner of any lesson). It wakes the voice up before replaying.
2. **Grown-Ups → 🔈 No sound? → Fix the sound.** Repairs the voice, says a test
   sentence, and tells you what it found.
3. **Check the iPad itself**, which the app can't see or change: the silent-mode
   bell in Control Center (swipe down from the top-right — the voice obeys it even
   when videos still play), the volume buttons, and Bluetooth headphones.

Version 1.5 fixed four bugs that could each make the sound vanish on its own:
one failed line silenced every line after it; iOS paused the voice when the app
went to the background and nothing ever un-paused it; audio was only unlocked on
the very first tap; and Stop let lines that were already queued keep playing.
`chance/test/sound.js` reproduces each one with a fake iPad speech engine.

## Stop

**🛑 Stop** silences the talking instantly. Inside a lesson it sits in the bottom
bar next to Home. Everywhere else — the Word Wall, the menu, the grown-up area —
a 🛑 Stop button floats up at the top the moment the app starts talking, and goes
away when it's quiet.

## Updating the app

The app caches itself so it works with no signal. That is the right call at the
creek and the wrong one the day a fix is published — otherwise the iPad happily
runs the old copy forever.

**Updates now arrive by themselves.** From version 1.6 the app checks the
server every time it opens (it falls back to the saved copy only when there is
no signal). When a new version has downloaded, it reloads itself the next time
Chance is on the home screen, never in the middle of a question. In practice,
close the app and open it again and the new version is there.

Before 1.6, the iPad could keep running old code even after reporting that it
had updated. That is how the first-grade words survived the grade-2 change.
If the iPad still shows old content, do the update by hand once:

**Grown-Ups → ⬆️ Update the app → Check for an update.** It says plainly
whether anything is new, and updating throws away the cached copy and reloads.
**His stars, badges, recordings and homework are all kept** — the update clears
caches only, never his data.

There is also **🧹 Force a fresh copy** for when you are fairly sure something
is stale and do not want to think about version numbers.

For whoever is publishing: bump `VERSION` in `chance/js/updater.js`, then run
`node chance/build-single-file.js`. The build writes `chance/version.json` from
that constant, so the two can never drift apart — if they did, the app would
either nag forever or never notice an update.

## Use your own voice instead of the computer's

The synthesized voice is a fallback, not the goal. For a kid this age the
voice *is* the teacher, and a robot reading a lesson about salamanders is not
the same as Grandpa reading it.

**Grown-ups → 🎙️ Use your own voice** opens a recording booth. It shows one
line at a time in large type; you tap ⏺, read it, tap ⏹, and it saves and
jumps straight to the next line. From then on your voice plays instead of the
computer's, everywhere that line is spoken.

Lines are grouped by how often he actually hears them:

| Group | Lines | Why first |
|---|---|---|
| 🦉 **Coach lines** | 21 | What the owl says when he picks a wrong answer. These repeat on *every day of the course* — about 20 minutes of recording changes the whole feel of the app |
| ⭐ **Praise & app lines** | 12 | "That's it. Good, careful thinking." "Mission complete!" Also constant |
| 📅 **Each mission day** | ~48 | The lesson itself — roughly 4-5 minutes of reading per day |

That is 2,933 lines if you ever did all sixty days, which nobody is going to do
in one sitting — and you don't have to. **Anything you haven't recorded keeps
using the computer voice**, so a half-hour on the coach lines is a real
improvement on its own, and recording a week ahead on a Sunday keeps you in
front of him.

Recordings are matched to the *text* of a line, so a line recorded once plays
everywhere it appears. The 🔤 **Letter sounds** group is the highest-value
recording in the whole app: a synthesizer physically cannot say an isolated
/f/, but you can. Record the letter sounds once (83 at the grade-2 level) and every word in all 60
days that uses them is fixed — and phonics sounds are stored separately from
words, so your stretched-out "sss" never gets confused with the word "sun."

Clips live in IndexedDB on that device. **Export all** writes every clip to one
file — back it up, or Import it on a second iPad so you only record once.

Recording needs Safari on iOS 14.3 or newer, and iOS asks for microphone
permission the first time.

### If you don't want to record anything

iOS ships better voices than the default. On the iPad: **Settings →
Accessibility → Spoken Content → Voices → English**, and download a *Premium*
or *Enhanced* voice (Ava, Allison, and Samantha are the warmest). They are a
large improvement over the compact default, they're free, and the app will pick
one up automatically — or you can choose it under Grown-Ups → Settings.

## Reading level: Grade 2

Chance is going into second grade, so the Read & Say words are a
**second-grade set**, built against the second-grade reading standard
(Common Core RF.2.3). Each week covers one skill:

| Week | Skill | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | long and short vowels in two-syllable words | sunset, sunrise, rotate |
| 2 | vowel teams ai, ay, ee, ea, oa, igh | halfway, between, float |
| 3 | r-controlled vowels in longer words | orbit, farther, Saturn |
| 4 | suffixes -er, -est, -ful, -less, -ly | powerful, quickly, weightless |
| 5 | -ed and -ing, with doubling and dropping e | hopping, hiding, swimming |
| 6 | oi, oy and ou | moist, enjoy, cloud |
| 7 | oo, au and aw | brook, launched, jaws |
| 8 | soft c, soft g, and -le endings | giant, gentle, nibble |
| 9 | prefixes un- and re-, three-letter blends | unload, restart, scratch |
| 10 | two-syllable words ending in -er, -le, -y | lever, handle, pulley |
| 11 | silent letters kn, wr, mb, and ph that says f | knob, wrap, thumb, photo |
| 12 | compound words | footbridge, sailboat, notebook |

Words are split **by syllable** (*pow · er · ful*) rather than letter by letter,
because reading in syllables is the second-grade skill. Every word is still tied
to that day's science, and every day's sentence and reading question were
rewritten to use the new words.

The first-grade words are gone from the app entirely; there is no setting
that brings them back. For extra practice on an easier day, scan the words he
brings home from school (see *Scan his homework*).

## The reading is phonetic, and he says it out loud

Each word is broken into the chunks he actually decodes. He can tap any chunk to
hear just that piece, tap 🐢 to hear the whole word sounded out and blended, then
hold 🎤 and **say it himself**.

### How a chunk gets said (and why the first version was wrong)

Speech synthesis reads *words*. It cannot say an isolated phoneme. The first
version of this app invented spellings like `fff` and `rrr` and handed them to
the synthesizer, so **far** came out as two disconnected noises instead of a
blend. Thirteen words were worse than that — they had a silent chunk and said
nothing at all.

It now works the way a reading teacher works, anchoring each chunk in a real
word the engine is guaranteed to pronounce:

```
far  ->  "far"                          (whole word first)
         "f, like in fan."              [f highlighted]
         "a-r says ar, like in car."    [ar highlighted]
         "Now blend it."
         "far"
```

Every token in that script is a real English word, so no engine on any device
can mangle it — and it is the same language he already hears at school.

The rules that keep it honest, all enforced by `Phonics.audit()` in the test
suite:

- **Every chunk that the synthesizer can't be trusted with** — any single
  letter, any chunk with no vowel — must have an anchor word.
- **The anchor is never the word being decoded.** "s-p, like in *spin*" teaches
  nothing while he is reading *spin*, so each entry carries a spare anchor.
- **No invented phonemes anywhere**, checked by pattern.
- **Short vowels always say "short a"**, because the letter name *a* is the long
  sound and would teach the opposite.
- **Ambiguous graphemes are listed per word.** The *oo* in "brook" is not the
  *oo* in "moon"; the *ow* in "down" is not the *ow* in "slow".

Pronunciation lives in `chance/js/phonics.js`, keyed by the spelling chunk — so
"ar" is right in *far*, *star* and *Mars* at once, and only has to be fixed in
one place. The word data holds only the spelling chunks, never a pronunciation.

**If you record the 🔤 Letter sounds group in your own voice, he hears a real
human /f/ instead of the anchor phrase** — which is the correct way to teach
this, and the one thing no synthesizer can do.

The app listens, compares what it heard against the target, and responds:

- Said it right → "Yes — *orbit*. You got it."
- Close → "Close! Listen to the sounds again," then it re-sounds the word.
- Different word → "I heard *orbit* as… the word is **orbit**. Let me break it
  up for you," and it walks the chunks again.

It is forgiving about homophones (*sun* / *son*) because the goal is reading the
word out loud, not spelling it. If the microphone is off, refused, or he is just
having a quiet morning, **👍 I said it** keeps the lesson moving — nothing is
ever a dead end.

Then he reads a full sentence (tap any word to hear just that word) and answers
one comprehension question about it. Those questions are the summer-school work
continued: the wrong answers are the traps a kid who is word-matching instead of
reading for meaning actually falls into.

## The evaluation tool: it grades the *reasoning*, not the answer

This is the part a classroom of twenty kids cannot give him.

Every wrong answer in the curriculum is **tagged with the thinking error that
leads to it**. There are no throwaway distractors anywhere in the 60 days —
each one is a mistake a real second-grader makes. When he taps one, the owl
responds inside the same second with coaching aimed at *that specific error*,
never with "wrong":

| He picked… | Because | The owl says |
|---|---|---|
| the biggest number | bigger-number bias | "You grabbed the biggest number. Big numbers aren't always the answer." |
| 7 instead of 12 | added when it was groups | "That looks like adding. This one is groups." |
| "more time" for faster | flipped the relationship | "You turned it around. Faster means the trip takes LESS time." |
| a word from the question | matching words, not meaning | "That answer copies a word from the question, but it doesn't answer it." |
| "the Moon wants to…" | animism | "Planets don't want anything — they follow rules. What rule is doing this?" |
| perimeter for area | around-vs-inside | "Are we walking around the outside, or covering the inside?" |

Seventeen of these habits are tracked. It also watches **how** he works, which
no answer key can see:

- **Answered before reading** — faster than the question can physically be read.
  The owl slows him down: "Whoa, speedy! Read it with me once, then choose."
- **Same trap twice in one sitting** — "That's the same trap as before!
  Remember what we said?"
- **Three tries on one item** — two wrong choices fade out and the reasoning is
  walked through, but he still taps the answer himself.
- **Long freeze** — the item is above his level today; the app offers to reread
  it and never shows a clock.

A 🧠 meter in the corner moves in real time, so he can see focus as something he
controls.

**None of it punishes him.** There is no red X, no score, no timer on screen,
no losing. Getting it right on the second look is called out as a good thing,
because that is what scientists actually do.

## The Thinking Report (the 👨‍👩‍👦 corner button)

Every tap is logged on the device, and the report turns that log into plain
sentences you can act on:

- Missions finished, first-try accuracy, typical think time, streak, words read
  aloud.
- **What his wrong answers are telling us** — the thinking habits ranked by
  frequency, each with a specific practice suggestion ("practice *faster means
  less time* with two real trips").
- **How he is working** — guessing, freezing, thrashing.
- **By subject** — first-try accuracy per domain, sorted weakest first, so you
  can see that he is sound on multiplication but soft on unit conversion.
- **Words to practice out loud** — any word that needed more than one try.
- **One thing to ask him at dinner** — the last mission's question, with the
  answer, so you can have the conversation without reading the whole lesson.
- A **plain-text summary** with Copy / Email / Text buttons, for a parent or his
  teacher.

Settings live here too: his name on the reward cards, the fallback computer
voice, the speaking speed, a microphone check, and a reset. The recording booth
opens from here as well.

## Rewards he can actually send to people

- ⭐ **a star** per stop, 💎 **a gem** for a perfect first-try mission
- 🗓️ **a weekly badge** at 5 mission days in a calendar week, named after that

## Privacy

Everything lives in `localStorage` on that one iPad. No account, no login, no
server, no analytics, nothing uploaded. Nothing leaves the device unless
somebody taps Share. Speech synthesis is built into iOS and runs offline; only
the microphone check needs a connection on some devices. Homework photos are
read on the iPad by a text reader that ships inside the app
(`chance/vendor/tesseract/`, the open-source Tesseract engine). The photo is
never sent anywhere.

## Changing the curriculum

The content is deliberately separate from the machinery. Three plain files, one
per unit:

```
chance/data/unit1-space.js     days 1-20
chance/data/unit2-woods.js     days 21-40
chance/data/unit3-forces.js    days 41-60
```

Each day is one readable object. Adding a day means copying one and editing the
words — no build step, no tooling, nothing to compile. `chance/data/curriculum.js`
holds the week themes, badge names and phonics progression, and documents the
schema at the top.

The one rule when you write new content: **every wrong answer needs a tag**
naming the thinking error behind it. That is what the real-time evaluation runs
on. The available tags are listed in `chance/js/evaluate.js`, each with the
line the owl speaks and the practice suggestion the report prints. A wrong
answer with no tag still works, but it gets generic coaching instead of the good
kind.

## Running the tests

```
npx http-server -p 8099 -s .        # from the repo root
node chance/test/regress.js         # the multi-file app
node chance/test/sound.js           # the sound-reliability checks
node chance/test/scan.js            # scan a worksheet photo into a lesson
BASE=http://127.0.0.1:8099/dist/chance-academy.html node chance/test/regress.js
```

It drives a real browser and checks the things that have actually broken
before: every day's content, the phonics rules, the exits, a full day
walkthrough, homework generation, the updater, and all 180 day/station screens
rendering. Run it before publishing.
